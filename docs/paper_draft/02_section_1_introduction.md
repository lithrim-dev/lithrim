## 1. Introduction

Evaluating LLM systems in regulated domains — clinical documentation, claims, scheduling, coding — is no longer a research curiosity; it is a release gate. By 2026 the field has converged on a recognizable consensus architecture for it: run **deterministic conformance checks** where the specification is machine-checkable, an **LLM-judge council** where the question is semantic, and a **human-in-the-loop calibration loop** to align the judges to expert labels. Shipped products now embody this directly — judge-calibration is a named feature (e.g. LangSmith *Align Evals*, Langfuse's calibration tooling), and the prevailing best-practice statement is some version of *"code catches what code can catch, judges handle semantic judgment, humans calibrate the judges."* ⟦firm-for-camera-ready⟧ We do not dispute this architecture. We observe that it has **two load-bearing dependencies the consensus does not examine** — and that both are where the real difficulty, and the real contribution, lie.

**The first gap: the deterministic validators have to come from somewhere.** The consensus treats "the deterministic check" as given. But on any non-trivial specification — an HL7 ADT^A04 message, a FHIR Claim, a structured clinical note — the conformance validator must be *authored*, and authoring it by hand against a living standard is precisely the bottleneck the consensus elides. The obvious move is to generate the validator from the specification with a capable model. We show this is **unreliable in a way the model's own confidence score conceals**: in our spike, a single high-confidence generation call produces validators that are **accepted 0/3** by a multi-case oracle — they ship self-reported `confidence:high`/`partial` while erring on **9 of 10** cases. The model is not uncertain; it is confidently wrong about whether its generated validator is correct. A single-call "validator copilot" therefore cannot be trusted to author the very checks the deterministic floor depends on.

**The second gap: the labels the loop calibrates against.** The consensus calibration loop tunes judges toward a small **human-annotated** set (commonly 30–50 expert-labeled examples). That set carries annotation drift and cannot, by construction, certify the *absence* of a defect. We instead build labels that are **true by construction**: each case is produced by a programmatic defect-injection recipe, so the defect, its location, and the contradicting source span are known exactly — enabling false-negative *and* false-positive measurement without an annotator in the loop.

**The categorical claim (unchanged from the locked outline).** A semantic LLM-judge council is **categorically blind** to a class of specification-defined structural errors, and this blindness **does not close with more judge accuracy**, because the judge reasons about *meaning* while the violation is a property of *structure*. Composing a generated deterministic validator with the council under a **worst-of** rule recovers that error class. The blindness is not a percentage ceiling to be pushed up; it is a category boundary. Our sharpest evidence is also a failure mode we name **silent confident certification**: on the HL7 ADT^A04 pack a tuned three-judge `gpt-4.1` council catches **0/28** structural defects while rating them at confidence **1.000** — and a deployed monoculture council unanimously *approved* **5 of 10** injected defects, every one of its 36 votes at confidence `1.000`, including a fabricated diabetes diagnosis in a note whose transcript explicitly enumerated the patient's history without it. The certification is maximally confident and **invisible at the API surface**. The generated conformance validator catches **28/28** (+100 pp) under worst-of; the structural axis is recovered.

**What this is not.** It is not "a better judge." Reward-model scoring (e.g. Composo) and judge-noise reduction (criteria injection + ensembling, after Lail & Markham) both operate *on the semantic axis* — they make the judge more reliable at the thing judges do. Our contribution is **orthogonal**: a *generated deterministic validator* that recovers a class the semantic axis cannot reach at any accuracy, gated by a by-construction oracle. This orthogonality is the point of the positioning, and it is why "the council is now well-calibrated" — increasingly true across the field — does not close the gap we measure.

**Why now.** The eval layer is consolidating: foundation labs are absorbing the tooling (the local-first OSS standard was acquired in 2026 ⟦firm-for-camera-ready⟧). As the *concept* of "deterministic + judges + calibration" commoditizes, the unexamined questions — *can you trust a generated validator, and against what labels?* — become the load-bearing ones. We establish a priority result on **bench-gated generative production of conformance validators**.

### 1.1 Contributions

1. **A bench-gated generative pipeline for conformance validators** — a generate→test→refine loop in which a multi-case, *by-construction* oracle (not the model's self-reported confidence) is the acceptance gate. We show the gate is load-bearing: single-call generation is accepted 0/3 and errs on 9/10 cases; the gated loop produces accepted validators. *(Full mechanism disclosure, per the locked disclosure decision.)*
2. **A synthetic, deterministically-labeled clinical artifact benchmark** — Synthea base records, pinned seed, programmatic single/multi-defect injection, labels exact by construction, clean negatives for false-positive measurement. Released as generator + frozen test set.
3. **The categorical-blindness characterization + the worst-of composition result** — the specification-defined error classes a semantic judge cannot detect at any accuracy, and the recovery a generated deterministic validator provides under worst-of (HL7: council 0/28 → composition 28/28).
4. **Silent confident certification, measured** — the council certifies missed structural defects at confidence 1.000; the confidence field is degenerate (one value, zero information) — quantified, not anecdotal.
5. **A complementary decision-vs-attribution result** — across four semantic packs (N=10, 1380 council invocations) the council reaches 0.730 ensemble accuracy with a **5.73×** gap between getting the *verdict* right and emitting the *correct taxonomy code* — with the product implication that one should surface the *evidence span*, not the code.

### 1.2 Scope discipline

The categorical claim is **demonstrated on HL7 conformance**. The four semantic packs are **baseline characterization** (judge accuracy, false-block, the attribution gap, run-to-run instability), **not** thesis validation — four of five packs have no meaningful structural-vs-semantic separation, and the scheduling pack's apparent recovery is orchestrator `artifact_judge` behavior, not the structural axis (§6/§7b). Semantic-*equivalence* judgment — where two structurally different artifacts are purpose-equivalent, or a structurally valid one is semantically wrong — is **named as future work, not solved here**. We report distributions over N=10 runs, not single verdicts, because single-run verdicts are not trustworthy at this layer.
